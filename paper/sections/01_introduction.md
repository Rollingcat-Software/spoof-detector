# 1. Introduction

Face authentication is now one of the most widely deployed biometric modalities — present in smartphone unlock, payment authorisation, banking onboarding, online proctoring, electronic identity verification, and increasingly in regulated remote-onboarding pipelines that fall under PSD2 strong-customer-authentication, eIDAS 2.0, and KVKK / GDPR identity-verification obligations. Each of these contexts assumes that the face presented to the camera belongs to a *live human being physically present in front of the device* — an assumption that is routinely violated by a spectrum of presentation attacks (PAs), from a printed photograph held up to the lens, through screen-replay of a stolen video, to virtual-camera injection of a real-time deepfake. In 2026 the cost of mounting a credible attack has collapsed: open-source face-swap models run on consumer GPUs at thirty frames per second, mass-market virtual-camera drivers (OBS Virtual Camera, DeepFaceLive, ManyCam) route arbitrary frame buffers into any application that consumes `/dev/video0`, and high-resolution AMOLED phone displays now reproduce face-region pixels with sufficient fidelity that the moiré and bezel cues classical FAS depends on are increasingly absent. The result is an arms race in which presentation-attack capability is advancing faster than the public benchmark suite that the academic FAS community uses to measure it.

Despite a decade of academic FAS research and six widely-cited benchmarks (CASIA-FASD, Replay-Attack, OULU-NPU, SiW, CASIA-SURF, CelebA-Spoof), three structural gaps persist between the published methods and the systems shipping in production today.

**Gap 1 — Per-frame bias.** Every public FAS benchmark reports per-frame accuracy. A face is presented, a single frame is classified live or attack, and the metric is recorded. But the deployment contexts that economically dominate face-PAD — proctoring, hosted KYC, remote-banking onboarding — give the attacker minutes to hours of camera time. They can hold a print attack steady for two seconds, briefly reveal their real face for one frame, then swap back. A per-frame benchmark records the correct classification on the real-face frame and calls it a near-pass; an operator sees a sustained spoof and calls it an attack. Our prior internal investigation of the production FIVUCSAS face-verification pipeline (FIVUCSAS internal tech report 2026-05-09, *Liveness & Anti-Spoof Investigation*; on file at the project repository) confirmed this gap end-to-end: a passive-only liveness defence configured to evaluate one frame per `/verify` call returned an `is_live = True` verdict on a recorded-video replay attack that any sustained-session detector would have flagged within seconds.

**Gap 2 — Single-modality models.** Most published methods are *image* methods — a CNN, transformer, or hand-crafted descriptor on a single frame. Temporal signals — blink rate, micro-tremor, remote photo-plethysmography (rPPG), screen flicker — carry independent evidence that no single-frame method can recover, but temporal and image methods are typically reported in isolation. Per-method paper comparisons inherit this single-modality framing and reward improvements within a modality rather than across them. We argue that the right architecture is *hybrid*: a fast single-frame discriminator handles the obvious print/replay attacks, and a battery of temporal analyzers handles the harder ones (active video replay, AR filter, deepfake injection), with both fused at decision time.

**Gap 3 — Anti-correlated signals.** Two textbook anti-spoof signals — Laplacian texture variance and Gabor-moire — are widely believed to differentiate real-face frames from printed or replayed faces (cf. [Galbally 2014]). We measured them, and on our calibration set they discriminate the *wrong way around*: spoofs scored higher than reals. The reason is mundane: a modern high-resolution OLED replay actually has *more* uniform local texture than a low-light real-face frame, and the Gabor moire signature requires the cross-sampling artefact between a coarse camera sensor and a coarser display — an artefact that high-PPI 2026 phone screens eliminate. Yet the literature continues to use both signals as positive evidence for liveness. We reweight them to 0.1 in our fuser; in a preliminary weight-sensitivity analysis this single change recovers most of the AUC gap between uniform and calibrated weights on the in-house replay sub-protocol (§5.3, §8.3 — precise delta deferred pending an independent weight-configuration benchmark).

**Gap 4 — Deployment topology.** Production face-PAD is overwhelmingly assumed to be a server-side service: the client uploads a video, a GPU-backed inference server runs the model, and a verdict is returned. That topology has hard structural costs: every uploaded frame is a data-transfer event subject to GDPR / KVKK consent, every concurrent session is a GPU rent line on the operator's cloud bill, and every server outage takes the liveness check down with it. For an in-browser identity check on consumer hardware, the right deployment topology is the *inverse*: the model runs in the browser tab, no frame leaves the device, no GPU is required, and the operator pays nothing per-session for inference. The literature has not yet addressed how to make a hybrid session-PAD engine — nineteen analyzers, fifteen liveness-proof axes, MediaPipe pose tracking, ONNX inference — fit inside a 173 kB WebAssembly bundle that runs at 6–9 fps on a mid-tier Android device. That is the engineering contribution we report below.

## Scope and Capstone Context

This work was carried out as the Spring 2025–2026 CSE4297 / CSE4298 capstone project at Marmara University, advised by Doç. Dr. Mustafa Ağaoğlu. It is a scoped engineering investigation, not a domain-breakthrough claim: we do not benchmark on OULU-NPU or SiW (both EULA-blocked at the time of submission — see §9.4) and the in-house calibration set is N = 43 (27 bona-fide + 16 attacks). The contributions below are therefore tied to (a) session-level architecture design with formal spoof-burst-dilution resistance, (b) a calibration-transfer safety finding about texture / moire anti-correlation, and (c) a client-side browser deployment topology — and the headline numbers (AUC 0.945 / 0.782) are zero-shot on two public datasets, to be read with the cross-dataset taxonomy caveat in §9.2 and the per-operator recalibration requirement in §9.3.

### Mapping gaps to contributions

We address each gap with a corresponding contribution: **Gap 1 (per-frame bias) → Contribution 1 (peak-sensitive session verdict, §4.4)**; **Gap 2 (single-modality discriminator) → Contribution 1 (hybrid twelve-analyzer architecture, §4.2)**; **Gap 3 (anti-correlated calibration signals) → Contribution 2 (texture / moire calibration finding, §5.3)**; **Gap 4 (server-side GPU topology) → Contribution 4 (client-side WebAssembly browser bundle, §10.1)**.

## Contributions

This paper makes four contributions:

1. **A session-based hybrid PAD engine deployed entirely client-side.** The production deliverable is a **TypeScript + WebAssembly browser bundle** (~173 kB ESM, 54 kB gzip) running on `onnxruntime-web` (WASM EP, optional WebGPU EP with feature-detected fallback) and `@mediapipe/tasks-vision`. It runs a per-frame discriminator (MiniFASNet ONNX from the UniFace training corpus) plus **nineteen analyzers in total** (the formal evaluation of §7–§8 uses the Python reference's 13-analyzer configuration — MiniFASNet + twelve auxiliaries; the browser adds seven net-new analyzers on top, §10.1) — image-level (device-boundary, texture, Gabor-moire, screen-replay), temporal (blink/EAR, rPPG, screen-flicker, micro-tremor, landmark-variance, temporal-motion, background-grid), MediaPipe-blendshape-derived (eyebrow motion, per-eye blink symmetry, gaze, expression dynamics), 3D-pose, behavioural-pattern, and optionally hand-presence + voice-activity + audio-mouth-sync — under a calibrated multi-class fuser. The session aggregator uses a **peak-sensitive verdict** (50/50 blend of mean and worst-decile per-frame liveness, Fig. 1) that resists spoof-burst dilution (§4.4) and a **fifteen-axis passive liveness-proof scorer** (185-pt ceiling) that decays naturally on stale signal, catching the live-then-static attack class mid-session without requiring any active challenge (§10.1). A Python reference implementation — the original prototyping platform, kept because calibration sweeps and dataset benchmarks run faster outside the browser — is calibration-equivalent to the browser bundle for the twelve analyzers present in both, and is what backs the formal evaluation in Contribution 3 below.

2. **An empirical anti-correlation finding** for Laplacian-texture and Gabor-moire on modern high-resolution capture: spoofs score higher than reals, inverting the textbook assumption. Reweighting them from 1.0 to 0.1 improved fused AUC over uniform weighting on the in-house replay sub-protocol in a preliminary weight-sensitivity analysis (§5.3, §8.3); the precise AUC delta is deferred to §8.3 pending an independent weight-configuration benchmark. We additionally report (§8.2, Table) that two further auxiliary analyzers calibrated on in-house captures *harm* zero-shot performance on CASIA-FASD, in metric-dependent ways: removing `device_boundary` *improves* ACER by 2.69 pp and AUC by 0.027 (its presence harms both); removing `micro_tremor` *improves* AUC by 0.021 (its presence harms AUC, though removing it raises ACER by 3.60 pp). A third in-house-calibrated analyzer, `background_grid`, is a marginal *positive* contributor on this dataset — removing it *reduces* AUC by 0.014 with no ACER change. The operational implication of the two-analyzer harm finding is that any party deploying a calibrated FAS pipeline across capture domains must re-run the calibration sweep per-operator dataset. The same coefficients ship in both the browser bundle and the Python reference so this finding transfers without modification.

3. **Bootstrap-CI ISO/IEC 30107-3 evaluation on public datasets**, performed against the Python reference implementation. We evaluate the pipeline zero-shot on two HuggingFace-mirrored public benchmarks — CASIA-FASD (N=2,408) and CelebA-Spoof (N=2,611) — and on an in-house replay sub-protocol (N=100), reporting APCER / BPCER / ACER / EER / AUC with stratified-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. Headline AUCs are 0.945 [0.937, 0.956] on CASIA-FASD and 0.782 [0.766, 0.799] on CelebA-Spoof; the two confidence intervals are strictly separated by more than 4× their width, empirically nailing the cross-dataset taxonomy effect at 95% confidence (§7.1). The browser bundle inherits these numbers via calibration equivalence on the twelve analyzers present in both; the seven additional analyzers it introduces (blendshape-derived, 3D-pose, behavioural-pattern) extend the multi-signal evidence basis without modifying any Python-side weight, threshold, or test.

4. **An open-source, fully reproducible release.** `spoof-detector` ships under MIT license with the browser bundle published as `@rollingcat/spoof-detector` on npm and a live deployed reference at `https://amispoof.fivucsas.com/` running entirely client-side in any modern browser. The accompanying Python reference (138 unit tests on the calibration / fusion / session layer) plus the TypeScript bundle (217 unit tests on the browser-side analyzers and proof scorer, all CI-gated) back a benchmark harness implementing the ISO/IEC 30107-3 metric set with bootstrap CIs (`tests/benchmark/run.py`, `src/metrics/iso30107.py`), HuggingFace dataset adapters for CASIA-FASD / CelebA-Spoof / OULU-NPU / SiW / CASIA-SURF, and an iBeta PAD-Level-1 submission package (`iBeta_PAD_Level1_Submission_Package.md`) — every cell pinned to a release commit, no fabricated numbers, no proprietary cells.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. §2 surveys the FAS literature across five strands (classical hand-crafted, deep CNN, public benchmarks and ISO metrics, session-level and temporal aggregation, AR-filter and deepfake-injection). §3 defines the 7-category spoof taxonomy that drives our fusion. §4 presents the system architecture: image-level analyzers, temporal analyzers, multi-class fusion, peak-sensitive session aggregation, and the optional active-challenge layer. §5 details the calibration methodology and the anti-correlation finding. §6 describes the benchmark harness, dataset adapters, and protocols. §7 reports the headline cross-dataset results with bootstrap CIs. §8 ablates each design choice and includes the cross-dataset harm finding. §9 discusses limitations, open problems, and the path from PAD Level 1 toward Level 2. §10 concludes.
