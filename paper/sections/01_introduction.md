# 1. Introduction

Face authentication is now one of the most widely deployed biometric modalities — present in smartphone unlock, payment authorisation, banking onboarding, online proctoring, electronic identity verification, and increasingly in regulated remote-onboarding pipelines that fall under PSD2 strong-customer-authentication, eIDAS 2.0, and KVKK / GDPR identity-verification obligations. Each of these contexts assumes that the face presented to the camera belongs to a *live human being physically present in front of the device* — an assumption that is routinely violated by a spectrum of presentation attacks (PAs), from a printed photograph held up to the lens, through screen-replay of a stolen video, to virtual-camera injection of a real-time deepfake. In 2026 the cost of mounting a credible attack has collapsed: open-source face-swap models run on consumer GPUs at thirty frames per second, mass-market virtual-camera drivers (OBS Virtual Camera, DeepFaceLive, ManyCam) route arbitrary frame buffers into any application that consumes `/dev/video0`, and high-resolution AMOLED phone displays now reproduce face-region pixels with sufficient fidelity that the moiré and bezel cues classical FAS depends on are increasingly absent. The result is an arms race in which presentation-attack capability is advancing faster than the public benchmark suite that the academic FAS community uses to measure it.

Despite a decade of academic FAS research and five widely-cited benchmarks (CASIA-FASD, Replay-Attack, OULU-NPU, SiW, CASIA-SURF, CelebA-Spoof), three structural gaps persist between the published methods and the systems shipping in production today.

**Gap 1 — Per-frame bias.** Every public FAS benchmark reports per-frame accuracy. A face is presented, a single frame is classified live or attack, and the metric is recorded. But the deployment contexts that economically dominate face-PAD — proctoring, hosted KYC, remote-banking onboarding — give the attacker minutes to hours of camera time. They can hold a print attack steady for two seconds, briefly reveal their real face for one frame, then swap back. A per-frame benchmark records the correct classification on the real-face frame and calls it a near-pass; an operator sees a sustained spoof and calls it an attack. Our prior internal investigation of the production FIVUCSAS face-verification pipeline ([LIVENESS_ANTISPOOF_INVESTIGATION_2026-05-09]) confirmed this gap end-to-end: a passive-only liveness defence configured to evaluate one frame per `/verify` call returned an `is_live = True` verdict on a recorded-video replay attack that any sustained-session detector would have flagged within seconds.

**Gap 2 — Single-modality models.** Most published methods are *image* methods — a CNN, transformer, or hand-crafted descriptor on a single frame. Temporal signals — blink rate, micro-tremor, remote photo-plethysmography (rPPG), screen flicker — carry independent evidence that no single-frame method can recover, but temporal and image methods are typically reported in isolation. Per-method paper comparisons inherit this single-modality framing and reward improvements within a modality rather than across them. We argue that the right architecture is *hybrid*: a fast single-frame discriminator handles the obvious print/replay attacks, and a battery of temporal analyzers handles the harder ones (active video replay, AR filter, deepfake injection), with both fused at decision time.

**Gap 3 — Anti-correlated signals.** Two textbook anti-spoof signals — Laplacian texture variance and Gabor-moire — are widely believed to differentiate real-face frames from printed or replayed faces (cf. [Galbally 2014]). We measured them, and on our calibration set they discriminate the *wrong way around*: spoofs scored higher than reals. The reason is mundane: a modern high-resolution OLED replay actually has *more* uniform local texture than a low-light real-face frame, and the Gabor moire signature requires the cross-sampling artefact between a coarse camera sensor and a coarser display — an artefact that high-PPI 2026 phone screens eliminate. Yet the literature continues to use both signals as positive evidence for liveness. We reweight them to 0.1 in our fuser; this single change recovers most of the AUC gap between uniform and calibrated weights on the in-house replay sub-protocol (§5.3, §8.3).

## Contributions

This paper makes four contributions:

1. **A session-based hybrid PAD engine** combining one per-frame discriminator (MiniFASNet ONNX from the UniFace training corpus) with twelve auxiliary analyzers — four image-level (device-boundary, AR-filter, texture, Gabor-moire) and eight temporal (blink/EAR, rPPG, screen-replay, micro-tremor, landmark-variance, temporal-motion, background-grid, screen-flicker) — under a calibrated multi-class fuser. The session aggregator uses a **peak-sensitive verdict** (50/50 blend of mean and worst-decile per-frame liveness) that resists spoof-burst dilution and is the property we argue session-level FAS needs but per-frame FAS cannot provide (§4.4).

2. **An empirical anti-correlation finding** for Laplacian-texture and Gabor-moire on modern high-resolution capture: spoofs score higher than reals, inverting the textbook assumption. Reweighting them from 1.0 to 0.1 alone recovers 0.017 of the 0.019 AUC gap between uniform and calibrated fusers on the in-house replay sub-protocol (§5.3, §8.3). We additionally report that three further auxiliary analyzers — device-boundary, micro-tremor, background-grid — calibrated on in-house captures *actively harm* zero-shot performance on CASIA-FASD by ≈ 2.7, 3.6, and 1.4 pp respectively (§8.2), a result with direct operational implications for any party deploying a calibrated FAS pipeline across capture domains.

3. **Bootstrap-CI ISO/IEC 30107-3 evaluation on public datasets.** We evaluate the productized pipeline zero-shot on two HuggingFace-mirrored public benchmarks — CASIA-FASD (N=2,408) and CelebA-Spoof (N=2,611) — and on an in-house replay sub-protocol (N=100), reporting APCER / BPCER / ACER / EER / AUC with stratified-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. Headline AUCs are 0.945 [0.937, 0.956] on CASIA-FASD and 0.782 [0.766, 0.799] on CelebA-Spoof; the two confidence intervals are strictly separated by more than 4× their width, empirically nailing the cross-dataset taxonomy effect at 95% confidence (§7.1).

4. **An open-source, fully reproducible release.** `spoof-detector` v0.2.1 — the canonical home for every liveness / anti-spoof asset across the FIVUCSAS programme — ships under MIT license with 139 unit tests, a benchmark harness implementing the ISO/IEC 30107-3 metric set with bootstrap CIs (`tests/benchmark/run.py`, `src/metrics/iso30107.py`), HuggingFace dataset adapters for CASIA-FASD / CelebA-Spoof / OULU-NPU / SiW / CASIA-SURF, and an iBeta PAD-Level-1 submission package (`iBeta_PAD_Level1_Submission_Package.md`) that pins the v0.2.1 release commit and reports every cell exactly as it appears in the paper sources — no fabricated numbers, no proprietary cells.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. §2 surveys the FAS literature across five strands (classical hand-crafted, deep CNN, public benchmarks and ISO metrics, session-level and temporal aggregation, AR-filter and deepfake-injection). §3 defines the 7-category spoof taxonomy that drives our fusion. §4 presents the system architecture: image-level analyzers, temporal analyzers, multi-class fusion, peak-sensitive session aggregation, and the optional active-challenge layer. §5 details the calibration methodology and the anti-correlation finding. §6 describes the benchmark harness, dataset adapters, and protocols. §7 reports the headline cross-dataset results with bootstrap CIs. §8 ablates each design choice and includes the cross-dataset harm finding. §9 discusses limitations, open problems, and the path from PAD Level 1 toward Level 2. §10 concludes.
