# 2. Related Work

Face anti-spoofing (FAS), or presentation-attack detection (PAD) for the face modality, has accumulated more than fifteen years of academic literature spanning hand-crafted texture descriptors, deep convolutional networks, multi-modal sensing, video-temporal cues, and most recently, generative-model attribution. This section organises that literature into five strands — classical hand-crafted features (§2.1), deep learning-based PAD (§2.2), public benchmarks and metrics (§2.3), session and temporal aggregation (§2.4), and AR-filter / deepfake-injection detection (§2.5) — and at the close of each strand contrasts the design assumptions of the cited work against the engine introduced in §4.

## 2.1 Classical hand-crafted feature FAS

The first generation of FAS systems treated presentation-attack detection as a binary texture-classification problem. [Maatta 2011] introduced multi-scale Local Binary Patterns (MS-LBP) on greyscale face crops and demonstrated that the printed-photograph artefacts of inkjet halftone, paper-fibre micro-texture, and rephotograph-induced blur are linearly separable from bona-fide skin texture in a dense LBP feature space — the foundational result that justified texture-only FAS for almost a decade. [Boulkenafet 2016] extended the same descriptor family to the YCbCr and HSV colour spaces ("Color-LBP") and combined LBP with Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF), delivering a substantial accuracy lift on the then-standard CASIA-FASD and Replay-Attack benchmarks; the Color-LBP+SURF baseline remained competitive into the deep-learning era and is still routinely reported as a non-deep reference row in modern leaderboards.

A parallel hand-crafted strand attacked the temporal axis directly. [Pan 2007] proposed eye-blink detection on Conditional Random Fields trained over closed/open eye states and showed that the prevalence of blinks in a short video clip is itself a strong liveness signal — an observation that motivated every subsequent challenge-response liveness scheme. [Bharadwaj 2013] applied Eulerian video motion magnification to amplify sub-pixel skin micro-motion that printed photographs cannot reproduce; the magnified-motion energy in specific frequency bands proved discriminative without requiring any active user cooperation. [Tirunagari 2015] introduced Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) for FAS, framing presentation-attack detection as a problem of identifying temporal eigenmodes that distinguish a static spoof presentation from a live captured face. The DMD framework is ancestral to the rPPG and micro-tremor analyzers we describe in §4.2 — both isolate narrow-band frequency content as evidence of biological presence.

Frequency-domain texture analysis forms a third hand-crafted strand. [Galbally 2014] surveyed image-quality metrics across 25 hand-crafted descriptors, including specular reflection, blur, contrast, and the radial-FFT moiré signature that manifests when a screen is rephotographed by another digital sensor. The Galbally survey is widely cited as the canonical motivation for both moiré-based and texture-based FAS analyzers — and is, transparently, the literature lineage that bequeathed the Laplacian-texture and Gabor-moiré analyzers we report as anti-correlated on real-world capture data in §1 and §5. The signals are real, but their *sign* on modern high-resolution capture pipelines is no longer the sign assumed in the 2014 survey, a finding we believe is unique to this paper.

In contrast, the engine introduced in §4 retains MS-LBP-style and FFT-style descriptors only as low-weight interpretability columns (texture and moiré at 0.1 each in `src/infrastructure/fusion/multi_class_fuser.py:26-40`). The dominant per-frame discriminator in our pipeline is a CNN, not a hand-crafted descriptor; the hand-crafted descriptors survive in the pipeline as auditable evidence channels rather than as primary classifiers.

## 2.2 Deep CNN-based FAS

The deep-learning generation of FAS opened with end-to-end binary CNNs trained on RGB face crops, but the field rapidly recognised that auxiliary supervision — depth maps, rPPG signals, binary masks — improves generalisation across capture domains. [Liu 2018] (CVPR) introduced auxiliary depth and rPPG supervision in the "Silent-Face" architecture and showed that the depth-map auxiliary head, even when learned from monocular RGB, dramatically tightens cross-dataset generalisation versus pure binary supervision. [Yu 2020] (CVPR) replaced standard convolutions with a Central-Difference Convolution (CDCN) that explicitly encodes intensity gradients in the receptive field; CDCN posted state-of-the-art results on OULU-NPU's four protocols and remains a frequently-cited reference architecture for image-only FAS. [Wang 2020] (CVPR) added explicit spatio-temporal modelling via FAS-SGTD (Spatio-temporal Gradient Detection), showing that the gradient signal across consecutive frames carries discrimination unavailable to single-frame CDCN. [Liu 2020a] introduced disentangled FAS via the Spoof Trace Disentanglement Network (STDN), framing PAD as the recovery of an additive "spoof trace" that, once subtracted, returns a bona-fide face — useful both as a classifier and as a generative residual visible to operators.

A separate deep strand pursued lightweight architectures for mobile and embedded deployment. [Liu 2020b] introduced MiniFASNet, a MobileNet-style backbone trained on a curated mixture of public PAD corpora with the explicit constraint of fitting in <2 MB of weights and running at ≥100 FPS on commodity ARM CPUs. The MiniFASNet weights packaged inside the open-source `uniface` Python library are the per-frame discriminator at the heart of our pipeline (`src/infrastructure/analyzers/minifasnet_analyzer.py`); we use them zero-shot, without fine-tuning, in every result reported in §7. [Yu 2021] further automated the architecture search step itself with NAS-FAS, deriving FAS-specific cells via differentiable architecture search and improving cross-dataset ACER on OULU-NPU P4 by an additional several percentage points over CDCN.

Vision-transformer-era contributions include ViT-FAS variants and CLIP-style contrastive PAD, but those works are recent enough that the cross-dataset numbers reported are not yet directly comparable to the CNN leaderboards of OULU-NPU and SiW. We therefore restrict the deep-FAS comparison in §7 to CDCN, FAS-SGTD, Silent-Face, and MiniFASNet — the architectures with the most reproducible benchmark protocols.

In contrast, the engine in §4 does not propose a new CNN architecture. It re-uses MiniFASNet (weight 5.0 in our fuser, the largest of any analyzer) and surrounds it with twelve auxiliary analyzers — seven temporal and five image-level non-CNN — under a calibrated multi-class voter. This mirrors the "auxiliary supervision" thesis of Silent-Face but at decision-fusion time rather than training time, allowing operators to swap analyzers without retraining the dominant CNN.

## 2.3 Public benchmarks and the ISO/IEC 30107-3 metric set

A FAS benchmark is defined by its capture protocol, its presentation-attack instrument (PAI) taxonomy, and its evaluation metric set. The five canonical benchmarks of the modern FAS literature are CASIA-FASD, Replay-Attack, OULU-NPU, SiW, CASIA-SURF, and CelebA-Spoof; we evaluate against the latter four (and a HuggingFace mirror of the first) in §7.

[Zhang 2012] introduced CASIA-FASD at ICB, with 50 subjects and three attack categories — warped photo, cut photo, and video replay. Although superseded by larger benchmarks, CASIA-FASD remains the default cross-dataset *evaluation* target because its capture conditions diverge sharply from any modern training corpus; the akahana HuggingFace mirror (`tests/benchmark/datasets/casia_fasd.py:1-48`) flattens its three-class structure to bonafide/attack but preserves the cross-dataset robustness signal. [Chingovska 2012] introduced Replay-Attack at IDIAP, paired with the Spoofing-Database protocol and an LBP-based baseline; Replay-Attack is the historical reference for camera-quality replay attacks. [Boulkenafet 2017] introduced OULU-NPU at FG with 55 subjects, six phone models, three sessions, and five PAI species (one bonafide + two paper-print + two monitor-replay), and defined the four protocols — P1 cross-session, P2 cross-PAI, P3 cross-camera, P4 full cross — that have become the de-facto standard for FAS robustness reporting (`tests/benchmark/datasets/oulu_npu.py:1-39`). [Liu 2018] introduced SiW alongside the Silent-Face architecture, with 165 subjects under varied lighting, pose, and capture-distance conditions and a binary live/spoof + 2-paper-type spoof taxonomy (`tests/benchmark/datasets/siw.py:1-23`).

[Zhang 2019] introduced CASIA-SURF at CVPR Workshops with 1,000 subjects and synchronised RGB+depth+IR streams, opening the multi-modal FAS line; we consume only the RGB modality for parity with our single-camera deployment but record depth/IR paths in adapter metadata for future cross-modal experiments (`tests/benchmark/datasets/casia_surf.py:1-41`). [Zhang 2020] introduced CelebA-Spoof at ECCV with 625k images across 10 spoof types and 43 attribute annotations — the most diverse FAS benchmark by attack taxonomy and the only public corpus that explicitly labels `face_mask`, `upper_body_mask`, `region_mask`, and `3d_mask` species (`tests/benchmark/datasets/celeba_spoof.py:27-39`). [Li 2018] released ROSE-YOUTU, a smaller in-the-wild collection focused on social-network compression artefacts and consumer-grade replay attacks.

Beyond the benchmarks themselves, the FAS field has converged on the [ISO/IEC 30107-3 2017] metric set: APCER (Attack Presentation Classification Error Rate, per attack type then maximised), BPCER (Bona-fide Presentation Classification Error Rate), ACER (Average of APCER and BPCER), EER (Equal Error Rate), and AUC. These metrics replaced the earlier and operationally-confusing "false-acceptance rate / false-rejection rate" formulation by anchoring presentation-attack reporting to the per-attack-type worst case rather than the global average, which had previously masked species-specific failure modes. Our reference implementation is `src/metrics/iso30107.py` with twelve unit tests.

In contrast, the present paper does not propose a new benchmark. It proposes a *protocol composition* — applying the existing OULU-NPU / SiW / CASIA-SURF / CelebA-Spoof / CASIA-FASD adapters and a synthesised in-house calibration set as five lenses on the same productized pipeline, reporting the ISO/IEC 30107-3 metric set on each lens. The benchmark harness (`tests/benchmark/run.py`) is open-sourced under MIT.

## 2.4 Session-level and temporal-aggregation FAS

A small but instructive substream of the FAS literature treats PAD as a sequential decision problem rather than a per-frame one. [Tan 2010] proposed Temporal-LBP (T-LBP), extending LBP into the time axis to capture short-window texture stability, and showed that a 16-frame T-LBP descriptor outperforms its single-frame counterpart on Replay-Attack. [Pereira 2014] extended this with multi-scale temporal LBP, varying the analysis window from 4 to 32 frames and demonstrating that different attack species are best discriminated at different temporal scales — print attacks at long windows, replay attacks at short windows — anticipating the multi-band session-aggregation logic we describe in §4.4. [Bharadwaj 2013], cited in §2.1, also belongs structurally to this strand.

Industrial applications — particularly online proctoring, hosted KYC, and high-stakes authentication — generate the strongest demand for *session-level* PAD: a verdict that aggregates evidence across an entire user-camera interaction, not just a single sampled frame. The proctoring-systems literature (Examity, ProctorU, Honorlock and similar platforms) has published comparatively little peer-reviewed FAS work; what little exists is dominated by short technical reports and white papers rather than peer-reviewed FAS benchmarks, despite proctoring being the most economically significant deployment context for face-PAD outside smartphone unlock. The mismatch between the operational importance of session-level FAS and the academic-publication footprint of session-level FAS is a notable gap in the literature; it is a gap we explicitly address with the peak-sensitive session aggregator described in §4.4.

In contrast, the present paper introduces a 50/50 mean-plus-worst-decile session aggregator that explicitly resists spoof-burst dilution — the failure mode where a long mostly-real session masks a brief but adversarial spoof window. We are not aware of prior FAS work that publishes an explicit aggregator with this property under ISO/IEC 30107-3 metrics on multiple public benchmarks.

## 2.5 AR-filter, GAN, and deepfake-injection detection

Generative-model presentation attacks are the newest entrant to the FAS taxonomy. [Rossler 2019] (ICCV) introduced FaceForensics++, a 1.8M-frame benchmark of four manipulation methods (Face2Face, FaceSwap, DeepFakes, NeuralTextures) and demonstrated that deep classifiers trained on one manipulation method generalise poorly to others — the cross-method generalisation problem that still dominates the deepfake-detection literature. [Dolhansky 2020] (Facebook AI / Microsoft / Partnership on AI) released the Deepfake Detection Challenge (DFDC) dataset with 100k clips and ran the largest open competition to date; the headline finding was that even the winning ensemble degraded sharply on out-of-domain manipulations, underlining that deepfake detectors trained on a fixed generator family cannot robustly catch novel ones.

A related but distinct attack class is *injection*: the deepfake or AR-filter face is rendered locally and substituted into the camera buffer via a virtual webcam driver (DeepFaceLive, OBS Virtual Camera, or specialist tools sold on adversarial marketplaces). Injection bypasses every traditional rephotograph artefact — there is no LCD bezel, no scan-line beat, no moiré, no specular highlight — because the synthetic face never leaves the digital domain. The FAS literature on injection-class attacks specifically is sparse; most published deepfake-detection work consumes already-encoded video and does not interrogate the camera capture path. We treat injection as a distinct taxonomy bucket (`SpoofCategory.DEEPFAKE_INJECT`, see §3) precisely because it neutralises the cues that defeat replay attacks.

The AR-filter sub-class — Snapchat, Instagram, TikTok, and similar live-rendering filters consumed via virtual camera or mobile camera composition — is even less covered in the academic FAS literature. AR filters are *not* deepfakes (no generative model substitutes the identity), but they are also not bona-fide (the captured pixels are post-processed by a non-trivial rendering pipeline that introduces boundary artefacts, colour-uniformity changes, and tracking-driven micro-jitter). To our knowledge, no peer-reviewed FAS benchmark publishes AR-filter-specific APCER, and no public FAS benchmark provides AR-filter-labelled training data at scale. Closing this gap is one of the explicit contributions of our 7-category taxonomy (§3): we treat AR_FILTER as a distinct class with its own analyzer (`src/infrastructure/analyzers/ar_filter_analyzer.py`) rather than collapsing it into REPLAY or DEEPFAKE.

In contrast, the engine in §4 includes a heuristic AR-filter analyzer in its image-level bank and two analyzers (screen-replay specular, screen-flicker FFT) that are designed to fire on rephotograph injection but stay silent on virtual-webcam injection — yielding a fuser output that distinguishes REPLAY-class attacks from DEEPFAKE_INJECT-class attacks even though both render to the same pixel buffer. The taxonomy-level distinction is propagated into the per-class APCER reporting of §7 and the per-class operator dashboard.
